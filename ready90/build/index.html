Chapter One

The Game Plan

Four pillars, four levels, one practical order. Start this weekend and build at a pace that works for your family.

Budget levels below are illustrative estimates from the supplied November 2026 brochure. Check current local costs and build at your own pace.

Level 172-Hour Kit · ~$150 · this weekend
Level 23-Week Starter · ~$250–$400
Level 390-Day Core · ~$1,600 total
Level 4Power Add-On · $600 to $5,000+
Order of operations

Prioritize immediate safety and shelter, then safe water, food, and essential power. Individual needs and hazards vary; use local emergency instructions to set your priorities.

  • Safety first: CO alarms and fire extinguishers protect the air you breathe.
  • Shelter second: one warm (or cool) room plan, blankets, sleeping bags.
  • Water third, then food, then power to make it comfortable.
Rules that save money
  • Store what you eat, eat what you store. Rotate stock and check dates.
  • Buy calm, not in a crisis. Shelves are best on a sunny Tuesday, not the day before a hurricane.
  • Count calories and gallons, not "servings."
  • One room, not the whole house. Powering one room is affordable. A whole house for 90 days isn't.
Level 1 in a single cart (~$150)

At least 12 gallons of water for four people · 3 days of ready-to-eat food · battery CO alarm · first aid kit · flashlights and batteries · hand-crank radio · phone power bank · small bills in cash.

Chapter Two

Water

For four people over 90 days, the baseline is 360 gallons. Store what you can and identify a reliable, safe supply for the remaining days.

1 galper person per day, drinking + basic hygiene
× 4 × 90people × days for a family of four
360 galbaseline for four people over 90 days; a filter needs a safe source

Bottled water (grab-and-go). 10 cases of 40 × 16.9 oz bottles is about 53 gallons (not 100, as some videos claim), roughly 13 days for four. Keep it off the floor, out of sun, away from gasoline and chemicals.

Refillable jugs. Five 7-gallon food-grade jugs = 35 gallons. Label the date and refresh home-filled water every 6 months. A 55-gallon barrel adds about 14 more days for four people (about 460 lb full, so place it first).

Hidden home reserves. Some water heaters contain 40–50 gallons, but capacity and safe access vary. Follow the manufacturer and local utility instructions; shut off energy before draining and avoid scalding. A food-grade bathtub bladder can add storage when filled with safe water before an outage.

A gravity filter plus a source. A filter does not create a water supply or remove every hazard. Evaluate the source and filter specifications; follow public-health treatment guidance. Do not drink swimming-pool water. Water contaminated by fuel or toxic chemicals cannot be made safe by boiling or disinfecting.

Make clear water safe6% bleach8.25% bleach
1 quart / liter2 drops2 drops
1 gallon8 drops6 drops
2 gallons16 drops12 drops
8 gallons2/3 tsp1/2 tsp

Use fresh, regular unscented bleach of the exact concentration shown, with no added cleaners. Settle and prefilter cloudy water. Double the dose for cloudy, colored, or very cold water; stir and wait 30 minutes. If there is no slight chlorine smell, repeat the dose and wait 15 more minutes. Follow EPA instructions.

Or boil it

Follow local boil-water notices. CDC advises a rolling boil for 1 minute, or 3 minutes above 6,500 feet. Cool naturally and store in a clean covered container. Boiling does not remove fuel or toxic chemicals.

Don't skip this: filters and bleach handle germs, not gasoline or pesticides. Never drink from sources near flooding or chemical runoff.

Eat in the right order
  • Hours 0–4 · the fridge. Keep the door closed. After 4 hours without power or a cold source, discard refrigerated perishables. Never taste-test food for safety.
  • Days 1–2 · the freezer. With doors closed, a full freezer holds cold for about 48 hours (24 if half full). Check temperatures and food-safety guidance; do not assume thawed food is safe.
  • Days 3–14 · the pantry. Whatever expires soonest goes first.
  • Day 15+ · the anchors. Rice, beans, oats, peanut butter, canned meat.
Low-fuel & zero-fuel cooking
  • Ready-to-eat meals: canned foods labeled ready to eat, nut butter, crackers, and other shelf-stable options. Plan for allergies.
  • Food thermometer: cook foods to their safe temperatures. Keep perishables properly chilled; improvised insulation is not reliable temperature control.
  • Outdoor propane camp stove. Outdoors only, always.
  • Two manual can openers. The one you have will vanish exactly when the power goes out.

The bucket trap

Many "1-year" food buckets count tiny servings. Divide total calories by 2,000 per person per day.

The freezer trap

A freezer full of steak is a 2-day plan, not a 90-day plan.

The "we'll cook it" trap

150 lb of rice needs water and fuel. Your food plan only works if your water and stove plans do.

Runtime depends on actual loads, cycling, inverter losses, and battery condition. These are illustrative energy estimates. Measure your devices, and arrange a clinician-approved backup plan for medical equipment.

The 90-day engine: the hybrid

Sun panels battery essential circuits. A compatible solar array can refill a battery, but output depends on sunlight, shade, weather, panel placement, and charging limits. A safely operated outdoor generator can supplement charging when needed. Calculate your own load, charging time, and fuel use.

Set it up the safe way
  • Circle your 4–6 essential circuits.
  • Licensed electrician installs a power inlet + interlock or transfer switch, with a permit.
  • Never backfeed with a "double-male" cord. It can electrocute a utility lineworker.
  • Test monthly. Follow the battery manufacturer's storage charge, ventilation, temperature, and charging instructions.

Chapter Five

Fuel & Gas Cans

The 90-day fuel reality check, and how to store gasoline and propane without turning your garage into a hazard.

0.5–1 galper hour, typical portable generator
× 4 hrs × 90a modest daily run for 90 days
180–360 galfar more than anyone can store safely

So the plan isn't "store 90 days of gas." Keep only quantities permitted by local fire rules, in approved containers and storage locations. Plan around solar and essential loads where practical. Check the generator manual for actual consumption; never assume a fixed fuel stock will last 90 days.

Gas can rules
  • Approved, labeled gasoline cans only. Never milk jugs or glass.
  • Fill on the ground, never in a truck bed or trunk (static).
  • Leave room for expansion.
  • Add ethanol-rated stabilizer at the pump, and run the generator a few minutes so it reaches the carburetor.
  • Label every can with the fill date.
Store & rotate
  • Away from living space: a detached shed is best. Never near a water heater or furnace flame.
  • Shade and steady temperatures.
  • Rotate: untreated ethanol gas can degrade in about 3 months; stabilized gas typically lasts around a year. Use only suitable, uncontaminated fuel within its storage life and follow vehicle and stabilizer instructions; dispose of degraded fuel through local hazardous-waste services.
  • Check local fire code limits. Keep an ABC extinguisher nearby.

Propane

A 20-lb tank holds about 4.7 gallons and doesn't go stale. Store outdoors, upright. Dual-fuel generators run on either.

Monthly 15-minute run

Start the generator monthly under load. Keep spare oil, a spark plug and the manual.

The forgotten tank

Keep cars half full in storm season: fuel, charger and a warm room on wheels (never idling in a garage).

Chapter Six

Safety

Protect people first. Plan for carbon monoxide, fire, medical needs, sanitation, and extreme temperatures. Follow evacuation orders and local emergency alerts.

COinvisible, odorless, and potentially deadly
20+ ftgenerator from doors, windows and vents
Every levelbattery CO alarm, plus outside sleeping areas. Test monthly.
Carbon monoxide symptoms: headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, "flu" that improves when you leave the house. Get everyone into fresh air and call 911. Never run a generator, grill, camp stove or vehicle indoors or in a garage, even with the door open.

Fire

ABC extinguishers in the kitchen and garage. LED lanterns instead of candles; a headlamp per person.

Medicine

Ask your doctor, pharmacist and insurer about 90-day fills. Spare glasses and hearing-aid batteries.

First aid

A real kit plus a tourniquet and a first aid book. Take a class.

Sanitation

No water pressure means no flushing. Two-bucket toilet kit, heavy bags, sawdust or litter, lids, sanitizer.

Heat & cold

One interior "warm room." Sleeping bags, wool blankets. Know your nearest warming or cooling center.

Cash & documents

Card readers fail with the grid. Small bills, plus copies of IDs and insurance in a waterproof bag.

Household chemical rule: never mix bleach with ammonia or other cleaners. One product at a time, windows open.

Chapter Seven

Family & Neighbors

One prepared house helps. A prepared street wins. The most valuable prep is a plan everyone already knows.

The family communication plan
  • Out-of-area contact everyone checks in with.
  • Two meeting spots: near home and outside the neighborhood.
  • Paper phone list in every backpack and car.
  • Hand-crank NOAA weather radio with USB charging.
  • Text, don't call, during disasters.
  • Kids know the plan and practice it.
Give everyone a job
RoleOwns
Water captainJugs, rotation, the filter
QuartermasterFood rotation, cart list, cooking
Power chiefBattery, generator runs, fuel log
Safety officerCO alarms, first aid, meds, lights
Comms leadRadio, contacts, neighbor check-ins

Kids love a title. A 9-year-old Safety Officer will test the CO alarms every month without being asked.

Build a neighbor pod (5–8 homes)
  • Skills inventory: nurse, electrician, mechanic, gardener, ham radio.
  • Shared gear: one chainsaw, generator and ladder can serve a street.
  • Check-in list: elderly, disabled, on oxygen or home alone.
  • Street map: shutoffs, a meeting spot and a group chat, set up before you need it.
Host a 60-minute "Ready90 Night"
  • 0:00 Pizza and why we're here
  • 0:05 Walk the chapters, one each
  • 0:30 Skills inventory + shared-gear list
  • 0:45 Group chat + check-in list
  • 0:55 Everyone picks one Level 1 item to buy this week
What to say to a neighbor

"Hey, we're putting together a simple plan for our street in case the power is out for a while: who has a generator, who's a nurse, who might need a check-in. No doomsday stuff, just neighbors. Want to come by Thursday for pizza and a 1-hour walkthrough?"

Chapter Nine

The 4-Weekend Build

Four focused weekends to make progress, find the gaps, and practice together. Adapt the pace to your budget and household.

Weekend 1 · Safety & water
  • Install CO alarms; test smoke alarms
  • Extinguishers, first aid, lights, radio
  • Bottled water; fill and label jugs
  • Find the water heater drain and main shutoffs
  • Write the family contact list
Weekend 2 · Food & cooking
  • Warehouse-club run for the anchor cart
  • Repack bulk food into sealed buckets
  • Date everything; set up a shelf
  • Camp stove, fuel, can openers
  • Cook one rice-and-beans test dinner
Weekend 3 · Power & fuel
  • Choose your power path
  • Book an electrician for the inlet + interlock
  • Gas cans, stabilizer, propane
  • Charge the battery; label essential breakers
  • Set up sanitation and the water filter
Weekend 4 · The lights-off drill
  • Simulate an outage with lights off; protect medical equipment and essential services
  • Cook, light and charge on backup only
  • Note every "we didn't think of that"
  • Fix the gaps
  • Invite the neighbors

Every month

Test CO and smoke alarms · exercise the generator per its manual · check battery charge · use and replace pantry items.

Every 6 months

Refresh home-filled water · inspect fuel and rotate according to its storage guidance · check meds and first aid dates · update go-bags.

Every year

Replace bleach and batteries · rotate canned goods · re-run the drill · update the neighbor pod.

The easy-to-remember trick

Choose two dates exactly six months apart, such as April 1 and October 1. Set a recurring six-month reminder: refresh water, inspect fuel, and test alarms.

The first 60 minutes of an outage

Check people first. Injuries, medical devices, neighbors on the check-in list.

Close the fridge and freezer. Tape a note on the doors.

Turn on the radio. How big, how long?

Fill food-grade containers only while the water is known to be safe; follow utility notices.

Unplug sensitive electronics to avoid surge damage.

Start backup power safely: battery indoors, generator outdoors only, CO alarms on.

Text your out-of-area contact: "We're OK. At home. Will check in at 6pm."

Smell gas? Get everyone out, don't flip switches, and call the gas company from outside. Poison Control (U.S.): 1-800-222-1222 · Emergency: 911.
